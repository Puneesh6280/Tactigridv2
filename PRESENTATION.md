# TAC-GRID — Four-Person Briefing Script

For a 15–20 minute brief to a senior officer. Written to be **spoken**, not
read out. Use your own words once you know the shape — the point of scripting
it is so nobody freezes, not so you sound rehearsed.

---

## Before anything: the rules of the room

**Two people at the laptop, maximum.** Four people crowding a screen looks
like a school project. The speaker sits or stands at the laptop; the other
three stand back and stay quiet. Nobody adds to someone else's section unless
asked.

**One person drives their own section.** Swap cleanly at the handoff. Practise
the swap — it's the bit that always looks clumsy.

**Nobody interrupts the officer.** If he asks a question mid-section, the
person speaking answers it or hands it to whoever owns it (see §6). Don't
have three people answer at once.

**Decide the running order now and don't change it on the day.**

---

## The split

| | Who | Owns | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lead** | Opening, the close, the ask, hard questions | 2 min + close |
| 2 | **Ground** | The map, real distances, routing | 3–4 min |
| 3 | **Inside** | The 360° tour, the AI readout, detection | 4–5 min |
| 4 | **Response** | Reaction analysis, the offline proof | 4 min |

The Lead speaks first and last. That's deliberate — it frames the brief and
closes it, and it means one person is clearly accountable in the room.

---

## Person 1 — Lead: the opening (2 minutes)

Don't open with technology. Open with the problem.

> "Sir, thank you for the time. What we'll show you takes about fifteen
> minutes.
>
> The problem we started with is simple. If you want to understand a site
> properly — how it's laid out, where the approaches are, what's covered and
> what isn't — you either walk it yourself, or you look at a sketch and a few
> photographs. Neither scales, and neither survives a posting.
>
> So we photographed the Cadet Training Wing from a hundred and eight
> positions, in three-sixty degrees, and built a tool on top of it. You can
> stand at any of those positions, look around, measure real walking distances,
> and get an automatic assessment of what each viewpoint offers tactically.
>
> One thing before we start, because it shapes everything else. This runs
> entirely on this laptop. No internet, and no imagery of the site leaves the
> machine. For a defence location, we didn't think a cloud tool was an option.
> We'll prove that to you at the end rather than just claiming it."

**Handoff:**
> "[Name] will start with the ground itself."

**Note to Lead:** if he interrupts in the first minute with "so it's Street
View?", don't get defensive. Say: *"That's the right question, sir — can we
come back to it at the end? We've got a specific answer."* Then make sure
Person 4 delivers it.

---

## Person 2 — Ground: the map and real distances (3–4 minutes)

**Screen:** Command Deck → **EXPLORE** on the CTW card → the map.

> "This is the wing. Every marker is a position we photographed and then
> placed at its real coordinates, so what you're looking at is the actual
> ground, not a schematic."

Give him a moment to orient. Don't talk over it.

Then the routing — **this is the credibility moment**:

> "The useful part is measuring between them. Let me take the Main Gate to the
> Rear Gate."

Do it on screen. **402 metres**, with turn-by-turn.

> "Four hundred and two metres, and that's along the paths that actually exist
> — not a straight line across the buildings. It'll give the turns as well."

**Now hand him the controls. This is the strongest thing you can do:**

> "Sir, would you like to pick two? Anything on that list."

Whatever he picks, it answers. An unrehearsed question answered correctly is
worth more than anything you'd planned to say. If it's a pair that isn't
surveyed, say so plainly — see the honesty note below.

Also mention, briefly, that markers can be added:

> "We can also drop our own markers — a gate that's kept locked, a stretch of
> broken wire, anything the photographs don't show."

**Handoff:**
> "That's the layout. [Name] will take you inside it."

**If asked "how much of the camp is covered?"** — answer straight:
*"A hundred and eight positions photographed, eleven with survey-grade
coordinates so far. Distances between those eleven are accurate. Extending it
is survey time, not software."*

---

## Person 3 — Inside: the tour and the AI (4–5 minutes)

**Screen:** **OPEN TOUR** at the Main Gate.

> "This is the Main Gate. You can look around from where the camera stood."

Pan slowly. Let it land. Then walk one hotspot:

> "And you can move between positions the same way you'd walk it."

**Now the AI readout — and read this bit carefully.**

The Main Gate assessment is **hand-written by us**, not model output. Do not
let him believe the AI produced it. Say so, and turn it into a strength:

> "The panel on the right is the assessment for whatever direction you're
> looking. Now — the Main Gate one you're seeing was written by hand. We wrote
> it ourselves as a reference standard, so we'd have something to judge the AI
> against. Let me show you what the AI actually produces, so you're seeing the
> real thing."

**Move to Wing Office (node18).** It is the strongest genuine AI output —
every field populated. Read one or two lines aloud:

> "'The two-storey building on the left conceals a significant area behind it.'
> 'Trees along both sides can hide areas behind them — dead ground and
> concealment in various spots.'"

Then:

> "It does that for each direction — the structures and their openings, what
> would give cover, what only gives concealment, what's hidden from that
> viewpoint, the ways in, and the hazards. All hundred and eight positions have
> been done. It takes about a minute a position, on this laptop's graphics card."

**Object detection — keep it to 30 seconds, and only at the Main Gate:**

> "There's a second, much simpler tool that just picks out objects — people,
> vehicles, furniture."

Run it at the Main Gate: 2 people, a car, a bench, 2 potted plants.

> "That's a fixed list of about eighty everyday objects. It doesn't know what a
> door or a nallah is — that's what the written assessment is for. It's the
> weakest thing we've got and we'd rather tell you that than have you find it."

**Handoff:**
> "[Name] will show you the part we think matters most."

**DO NOT:** demo the AI readout at the Rear Gate (it's thin), and do not run
object detection at the Kote, the Gymnasium or the Central Anteroom — those
throw up false positives.

---

## Person 4 — Response: reaction analysis and the offline proof (4 minutes)

**Screen:** **REACTION ANALYSIS →** in the top bar.

> "Everything so far has been about understanding the ground. This is about
> time.
>
> Say something happens at the Main Gate. The question isn't how do I get
> there — it's who can get there, and how long does each of them take."

Run it from the Main Gate, walking pace.

> "Eklavya Mess, twenty-four seconds. Charlie Block, fifty-six. Wing Office,
> a minute and a half. The Kote, three minutes. The Rear Gate — just under five.
>
> So at walking pace, the Rear Gate cannot reinforce the Main Gate inside five
> minutes. That's not an opinion, it's the distance along the paths."

Switch to **DOUBLE** and let him watch the bands re-sort.

> "At double time it changes. That's the kind of thing you'd want to know
> before you decide where a reserve sits."

**Then the Google Maps answer** — he will have been thinking it:

> "Sir, the obvious question is whether Google Maps does this. For a public
> street, honestly, Google is better than us — better imagery, better
> directions. We're not going to pretend otherwise.
>
> But it doesn't have the inside of your camp, and it never will. It won't work
> with the network off. It has no idea what cover or dead ground is. And it
> can't answer the question we just answered — it does point to point, not
> 'who can reach this in time'.
>
> Google tells you how to get somewhere. This tells you who can get there in
> time, on ground Google's never seen, with no connection."

**The OPSEC close — the finale. Do it physically.**

Turn on flight mode where he can see. Reload. Keep going.

> "Network's off. The map's still there, the distances still work, the
> assessments are still there. The AI model runs on this laptop's own graphics
> card. Nothing about this site has ever been uploaded anywhere — and for a
> defence location, we thought that wasn't a preference, it was the
> requirement."

**Handoff:**
> "[Lead] will close."

## Person 1 — Lead: the close and the ask (1–2 minutes)

State the limits **before** he has to find them. It's what makes the rest
believable:

> "Three things we'd want to be straight about, sir.
>
> It's a planning aid, not a source of truth. It's describing photographs from
> one day, and it can be confidently wrong — anything it says needs a person to
> confirm it before it informs a decision.
>
> It does no fire planning. No killzones, no arcs. It'll tell you what would
> stop a round and what's hidden from view, but that's not the same thing and
> we don't want it heard that way.
>
> And it's eleven positions of a hundred and eight with survey coordinates.
> Extending that is time on the ground, not more software."

**Then ask for something specific. Never end on "any questions".**

Pick one:

> "What we'd like to ask, sir, is [permission to survey the rest of the wing /
> a decision on whether this is worth extending to another location / half an
> hour with your staff officer to work out where it would actually be used]."

## Q&A — who answers what

Agree this beforehand. Nothing looks worse than four people looking at each
other.

| Question about | Answered by |
|---|---|
| Why it exists, scope, cost, what happens next | **Lead** |
| Coverage, distances, accuracy, surveying effort | **Ground** |
| The imagery, the AI, what it can and can't see | **Inside** |
| Response times, offline, security, Google Maps | **Response** |

**If you don't know — say so.** *"I don't know, sir, I'd have to check."* A
senior officer has heard confident guessing before and doesn't rate it. One
honest "I don't know" costs nothing; one bluff that unravels costs the brief.

**If he pushes on something and you're wrong — concede immediately.** Don't
defend it. *"That's a fair point, sir, you're right."* Then move.

---

## If something breaks

Agreed in advance, so nobody improvises:

- **The person driving keeps talking.** Don't narrate the failure.
- **Lead steps in** with the next section while it's sorted, or moves to the
  fallback recording.
- **Never debug in front of him.** If it doesn't come back in about ten
  seconds, go to the recording and carry on.
- Have the **screen recording** of the full working demo open in another tab
  before you start.

---

## The morning-of checklist

- [ ] Password changed — do not log in as `admin123` in front of him
- [ ] Server running, logged in, map already loaded
- [ ] Tour open in a second tab at the Main Gate
- [ ] Reaction analysis open in a third tab
- [ ] Model warmed (open one assessment so it's loaded)
- [ ] Flight mode tested once, and turned back on
- [ ] Fallback recording open and ready
- [ ] Browser zoom 125–150%, notifications off
- [ ] One full run-through, out loud, with all four of you

---

## The five sentences, if it all goes wrong

If the laptop dies completely, one of you can still deliver this:

1. We photographed the site from 108 positions in 360°.
2. You can stand at any of them, look around, and measure real walking
   distances between them.
3. An AI on the laptop reads each view and reports cover, concealment, hidden
   ground, routes and hazards.
4. It tells you who can reach an incident and how fast — that's response
   planning, not navigation.
5. It all runs on one laptop with no internet, and no imagery ever leaves it.
